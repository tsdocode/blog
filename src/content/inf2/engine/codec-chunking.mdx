A codec call for all 32 streams has a large fixed cost and a small per-frame cost, so decoding 2 frames per call
every 2 steps is much cheaper than 1 frame every step:

| Frames per call (B = 32) | ms per call | ms per frame |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 19.7 | 19.7 |
| 2 | 27.9 | 13.9 |
| 4 | 48.5 | 12.1 |

The engine uses 2: most of the saving, with at most one step of extra latency.

## The cost: new streams wait for 2 frames

With chunking, a stream's first audio needs 2 frames *and* a codec step, so a new stream can wait 1–2 extra steps
(~60–130 ms) before its first audio byte — which is time to first audio (TTFA), the metric users feel.

## The replay trick

The codec decoder is causal: the audio for frame 1 depends only on frame 1 and earlier. So at a codec step:

1. A **new** stream with only 1 frame is decoded with a zero frame as padding. Its first 1,920 samples are exact —
   the padding cannot affect them — and are sent immediately.
2. Its codec state is now polluted by the zero frame, so the row is **reset**.
3. On the next codec call, the stream **replays** frame 1 together with frame 2 from the fresh state, and only frame 2's
   audio is sent.

The audio is identical to normal decoding: against a whole-sequence fp32 decode of the same frames, the streamed
output is at 46–48 dB SNR with exactly the same sample count, as before the change. Every new request gets its first
audio up to two steps sooner. (Implemented and tested for audio correctness; its effect on load-test TTFA is not
measured yet.)
