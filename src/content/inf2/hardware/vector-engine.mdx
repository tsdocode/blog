The Vector Engine handles operations where an output depends on **more than one input element**: adding two
tiles, a max over the free axis, a mask, a comparison, a reduction. It has 128 parallel lanes, one per
<T id="hardware/sbuf">SBUF</T> partition, each walking along the free axis of its partition.

## How it works

- Math is done in **fp32** internally; casting from bf16 on the way in and back on the way out is free.
- The parallel axis must be the **partition axis**. Lanes cannot see each other's data, except for limited
  shuffles and 32 × 32 transposes inside groups of 32 partitions.
- It reads <T id="hardware/psum">PSUM</T> directly, which makes it the natural engine to evict matmul results
  (bias add, residual add, mask add straight out of the accumulator).
- Using fewer than 128 partitions does not make an instruction cheaper — a `[32, N]` op costs the same as a
  `[128, N]` op. Fill all 128 lanes.

## What this means for kernels

The cost is linear in the free-axis length, plus a fixed overhead per instruction. Two consequences:

1. **Wide is cheap, many is expensive.** One op over `[128, 4 × 128]` costs much less than four ops over
   `[128, 128]`, because the fixed overhead is paid once.
2. **It is easy to make the Vector Engine the bottleneck** — it is the slowest-clocked engine, and every
   softmax, mask and residual lands on it. Offload what you can: element-wise single-input work belongs on the
   <T id="hardware/scalar-engine">Scalar Engine</T>, casts can go to <T id="hardware/gpsimd">GpSimd</T>.

Vector and GpSimd share SBUF access ports and cannot use them at the same time; Vector and Scalar likewise cannot
read PSUM at the same time.
